Flip Through Time · Episode 1

Before the fridge.

How did people keep food fresh with no refrigerator and no electricity? Flip went back in time to find out. Here's the full story.

No fridge, no electricity

For most of history, keeping food fresh was a daily problem. Meat, milk and vegetables spoil fast when they're warm, so families had to find clever ways to slow things down. There was no plug, no cold air and no freezer: just the kitchen, the seasons and a lot of know-how.

Salt and smoke

Salt was one of the most important tools in the kitchen. Rubbing meat and fish with salt pulls moisture out, and the bacteria that spoil food need moisture to grow. Smoking worked in a similar way: hanging meat in a smokehouse dried it slowly and added a protective layer of smoke. Many families also dried fruit, pickled vegetables in vinegar and made jams to get through the winter.

The cool cellar

The ground stays cool all year, so many homes had a root cellar dug underground. Potatoes, apples, cabbages, cheese and jars of preserves could last for months down there. Some farms also used springhouses, small buildings over a cold stream, to keep milk and butter cool.

Ice from frozen lakes

Then came a big idea: what if you could save winter's ice for summer? In the early 1800s, workers in New England began cutting huge blocks of ice from frozen lakes and ponds. The blocks were packed in sawdust, stored in thick-walled ice houses and shipped by boat and train to cities, and even to warm countries far away. Boston businessman Frederic Tudor, later called the "Ice King," sent one of the first shipments to the Caribbean in 1806.

Cutting ice was cold, hard and sometimes dangerous work. And in our story, that's exactly where things went wrong for Flip…

The icebox

By the late 1800s, many homes had an icebox: a wooden cabinet lined with metal and insulated with materials like sawdust or cork. A big block of ice sat in the top compartment, and the cold air sank down over the food below. The iceman delivered fresh blocks regularly, and someone had to empty the drip pan underneath, or the kitchen floor got wet.

The electric fridge arrives

Electric refrigerators for the home appeared in the early 1900s, but they were expensive and not very common at first. Models like General Electric's "Monitor-Top," introduced in 1927, helped make them more popular, and during the 1930s more and more families brought one home. No more ice deliveries, no more drip pans: just open the door and grab what you need.

Quick facts
  • Salting, smoking, drying and pickling kept food safe long before refrigerators.
  • Root cellars use the cool, steady temperature underground.
  • The natural ice trade took off in the early 1800s.
  • By the late 1800s, wooden iceboxes were common in many homes.
  • Electric home refrigerators spread during the 1920s and 1930s.
